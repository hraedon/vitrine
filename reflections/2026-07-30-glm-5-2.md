## On the project

The defining mechanic — provenance under glass, mechanically enforced — held up exactly as designed for a second country. `vitrine check` is the easy part: it caught nothing because the data is clean, and that's the point. The harder, more interesting tension only surfaced once I started transcribing: **the museum's spine is "the median-income four-person family," and most world-room sources measure something else.** The UK official series measure *households* and *individual earnings*, not families or household income. India's flagship survey measures *consumption*, not income. The `income-vs-consumption` and `composite-family` assumptions were written for exactly this, but living with them across seven rooms made clear how much of the world-room exhibit will be "render the gap" — and that that's not a failure, it's the honest exhibit. Most of my UK panels are gaps, and that's the correct museum.

The data-first / presentation-split continues to pay off: the data curation was completely unblocked by the presentation collision (WI-024). That separation is the reason "more data first" was a real option.

## On the work done

**Confident in.** Every UK fact is transcribed from a machine-readable primary source in `samples/` (ONS earnings xlsx, ONS tenure csv, GHS chart3 csv, terramededia licence HTML) — never recalled. All four source URLs curl-verified 200; the tenure CSV was confirmed byte-identical to the live ONS source. The macstudio OCR earned its keep as a *discipline check*: it transcribed the GHS chart fluently while misreading two cells (telephone 42→43, colour TV 61→60), which is precisely the OCR-skill calibration warning — I used the authoritative CSV. That cross-check is the kind of thing that prevents a wrong Tier-A number shipping.

**Judgment calls worth a second look:**
- The TV-licence facts (1950s/60s) are stored *without* a `quantity`, because a licence count is not a household percentage and would corrupt a %-based chart/stage axis. They render on the placard but gap in charts. This is honest but means the 1950s/60s diffusion panel is visually weaker than 1970s+. A reviewer might prefer I source a household-count denominator and compute the %.
- The pre-1970 wage anchor is *manual-worker* earnings (the all-employee ONS aggregate begins 1970), tiered with `manufacturing-wage-proxy`. So the 1950s/60s wage anchor is not strictly comparable to the 1970s+ all-employee series — a splice caveat the assumption ledger anticipates but each comparison must carry.
- I caught one copy-paste contamination before it shipped (Japan's "Work-Style Reform" had crept into a UK 2010s hours note). Worth noting because it's the failure mode the AGENTS.md "famous headline numbers" warning is about — contamination, not invention.

**Awkward:** the UK rooms have *no* affordability axis at all — no hourly wage (no hours data) and no household-income series, so no `wage_anchor`/`income_anchor` and no "work-buys" panel. Seven rooms where the signature affordability mechanic is entirely absent. That's an honest consequence of the archive, but it makes the UK rooms feel structurally different from the US rooms. Filling this needs LFS hours + an HBAI/FES income series.
